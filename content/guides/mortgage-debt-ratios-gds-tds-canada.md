# How Banks Calculate Mortgage Debt Ratios: GDS and TDS

Canadian lenders commonly evaluate mortgage affordability using two debt-service ratios: gross debt service, or GDS, and total debt service, or TDS. GDS compares qualifying housing costs with gross income. TDS adds other qualifying debt obligations to those housing costs. Together, they show how much of the income accepted by a lender is committed before ordinary living expenses and taxes are considered.

If you searched for a mortgage debt-to-income ratio, this is the Canadian framework you usually need to understand. Do not import a US calculator's definition or thresholds without checking its assumptions. This guide explains the inputs, works through a fictional application, and shows why a ratio that fits a benchmark can still leave an uncomfortable household budget.

## Use matching periods in the formula

GDS equals qualifying housing costs divided by gross qualifying income, multiplied by one hundred. TDS equals qualifying housing costs plus other qualifying debt payments, divided by the same income, multiplied by one hundred. Use monthly amounts throughout, or annualize every component. Dividing monthly bills by annual salary would produce a meaningless result.

For example, $96,000 of gross annual income is $8,000 gross monthly income. If qualifying housing costs are $3,000 a month, GDS is $3,000 divided by $8,000, or 37.5%. The equivalent annual calculation is $36,000 divided by $96,000. Both produce the same percentage because the periods match.

CMHC publishes maximum debt-service thresholds of 39% GDS and 44% TDS for its relevant insured lending framework. They are useful reference points, but lenders assess more than these two figures and product requirements differ. Passing a benchmark is not a borrowing entitlement, and an uninsured lender's policy should be confirmed rather than inferred from an insured example.

## Determine the income the lender accepts

The denominator is gross qualifying income, not automatically every deposit reaching your account. A transfer from savings, expense reimbursement, or borrowed amount is not ordinary earnings simply because it appears on a bank statement. Start with documented income and ask what evidence and treatment apply to each source.

Salary, hourly work, overtime, commissions, self-employment, benefits, and rental income may require different documentation or calculations. Do not assume that the highest recent month will be annualized. Ask the lender or broker to show the amount being used, especially after a job change, a leave, or a period with variable earnings.

For joint applicants, combine only the income accepted for the proposed application and include the corresponding obligations. Two people sharing expenses informally are not automatically treated as joint borrowers. A proposed rental suite also does not justify simply adding all hoped-for rent to salary; the lender's applicable rental-income method must be used.

## Build the housing-cost numerator

The core housing calculation includes mortgage principal and interest, property taxes, and heating. Applicable condominium fees and site or ground rent also need the relevant treatment. CMHC's published approach includes 50% of condominium fees and, for relevant chattel or leasehold loans, 100% of site or ground rent.

Use the mortgage payment calculated on the correct loan amount and qualifying assumptions. Include a financed insurance premium in the balance when required. If the tax bill is annual, divide it by twelve for a monthly worksheet. Do not add both an annual tax amount and its monthly equivalent to the same numerator.

Heating should reflect the subject property rather than an arbitrary number chosen to make the result fit. Request available records and ask how the lender estimates the amount when records are unavailable. A small unit with included heat and a large detached home can require different treatment, so identify what each fee actually covers.

## Distinguish the contract payment from the qualifying payment

The mortgage payment used for approval may be higher than the one calculated at the contract rate. Under the standard stress-test framework for a new applicable mortgage, the qualifying rate is the greater of the contract rate plus two percentage points and 5.25%. Confirm the rules for your lender, loan type, and transaction.

If a hypothetical contract rate is 4.5%, the corresponding qualifying rate is 6.5%. A worksheet using the 4.5% payment could therefore show a lower ratio than the lender's assessment. Keep the rate and payment labelled, and use the actual amortization and interest convention rather than guessing from a rough online quote.

The qualifying payment does not become the contractual bill merely because it appears in the ratio. Your daily budget starts with the contract payment, then adds a sensible stress scenario. Approval analysis and household cash flow are connected but different exercises. Using the right figure for each purpose avoids both overstating approval capacity and misreading the actual bill.

## Work through a complete fictional application

Consider accepted gross income of $96,000 a year, or $8,000 monthly. Assume the lender's qualifying mortgage payment is $2,350, property taxes are $350 monthly, and heating is $100. The property is a condominium with a $400 monthly fee, so this example includes $200 of that fee in the housing numerator.

Total qualifying housing costs are $3,000: $2,350 plus $350 plus $100 plus $200. GDS is therefore $3,000 divided by $8,000, multiplied by one hundred, or 37.5%. This fits the selected 39% reference point. The payment and property inputs are invented for teaching, not a quote or estimate for a particular home.

Now add a $450 qualifying vehicle-loan payment and a $150 qualifying revolving-debt amount. Other debt totals $600, making the TDS numerator $3,600. Dividing by $8,000 gives 45%. The housing ratio fits its benchmark, but the total-debt ratio exceeds the selected 44% reference point. One successful ratio does not cancel the other.

## Measure the gap instead of guessing

At $8,000 gross monthly income, a 44% TDS limit corresponds to $3,520 in combined qualifying obligations. The fictional applicant has $3,600, leaving an $80 monthly gap. Identifying that exact gap is more useful than assuming the mortgage is impossible or that a large extra down payment is automatically required.

If accepted other-debt obligations fell by $100 while housing stayed unchanged, TDS would become $3,500 divided by $8,000, or 43.75%. Alternatively, keeping all costs unchanged would require approximately $8,181.82 monthly accepted income, or $98,181.82 annually, under the TDS calculation. These are arithmetic comparisons, not recommendations or promises about lender treatment.

Any proposed change must be real and documented. A debt payoff may require proof and confirmation that the lender removes or adjusts the obligation. A future raise is not necessarily accepted income today. Recalculate both ratios after a change, because improving one constraint can leave another as the remaining limit.

## Count revolving debt using the applicable method

A credit-card statement's minimum payment is not necessarily the amount used for mortgage qualification. CMHC's published guidance calls for at least 3% of outstanding unsecured credit-card and line-of-credit balances as a monthly payment input. For illustration, 3% of a $5,000 balance is $150, which is the revolving amount used in the fictional example.

That does not mean every lender uses exactly 3% for every product, or that secured credit lines follow the same method. Different obligations can have different prescribed or internal treatments. Ask for a breakdown rather than applying one percentage to every balance on your credit report.

Also disclose obligations that may not be obvious from one report. The lender needs an accurate application, including relevant commitments outside the account you normally use. Hiding a payment does not make it affordable, and a calculation based on incomplete information is not a sound basis for a purchase decision.

## Compare debt repayment with a larger down payment

A larger down payment can lower the mortgage payment and may alter insurance costs. Paying off an existing debt can reduce the TDS numerator if the lender recognizes the change. The stronger option depends on the particular balances, required payments, premium bands, available cash, and which ratio currently limits the application.

Build two complete worksheets using the same savings amount. In one, increase the down payment; in the other, repay the selected debt and reduce available down-payment cash accordingly. Include any resulting insurance premium and preserve required closing funds. A comparison that spends the same savings on both choices is not feasible.

Then review interest costs and resilience alongside qualification. Removing a high-cost debt may have benefits beyond the ratios, while exhausting accessible cash can create new risks. Ask the lender to verify how each scenario would be underwritten before acting, especially where a small numerical improvement sits close to a product boundary.

## Identify expenses the ratios do not fully represent

The fictional condo owner must pay the full $400 fee even though the ratio includes $200. Home insurance, maintenance, nonheating utilities, groceries, transportation, childcare, and other household needs also require money. A ratio worksheet is a lending measure, not a complete list of what homeownership will cost.

Use actual take-home income for the personal budget. Subtract the contract mortgage payment and every relevant expense, including reserves for predictable future work. Do not use gross salary as if it were deposited without deductions. A household with high unavoidable nondebt expenses may need a lower mortgage than the benchmark calculation suggests.

Test timing as well as totals. Annual tax installments, a repair, or a gap between paydays can create a shortage even when average monthly spending balances. Keep sufficient accessible cash for the way bills actually arrive. Borrowing capacity is useful only if the resulting arrangement remains manageable outside the approval worksheet.

## Prepare an application you can reconcile

Gather income records, debt statements, down-payment evidence, and property-cost information. Create a dated worksheet showing each input and its source. Mark estimates clearly so they can be replaced when a specific property or financing offer is known. Keep the original version when assumptions change, which makes differences easier to explain.

Ask the lender or broker for the accepted income, qualifying rate, amortization, mortgage balance including premiums, housing allowances, and debt-payment inputs. If their result differs from yours, compare those fields one by one. A disagreement often comes from an input or policy difference rather than an arithmetic error.

Recheck before closing if finances or the property change. New borrowing, a changed balance, a different property fee, or revised employment information can affect the assessment. A preliminary calculation does not freeze the application forever. Keep the professional handling the mortgage informed and obtain clarification about outstanding approval conditions.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is DTI the same as GDS or TDS?

DTI is a general debt-to-income label used differently across contexts. For a Canadian mortgage, clarify whether the calculation means housing-only GDS or the broader TDS measure. Check the numerator, income definition, period, and thresholds. A percentage from another country's calculator may not be comparable even when the label sounds familiar.

### Do I use gross salary or take-home pay?

Use gross qualifying income for the lender ratio when that is the required denominator. Use take-home income for your household spending plan. Keep the two worksheets connected but separate. They answer different questions, and mixing their figures can create an apparently precise result that is wrong for both purposes.

### Can a strong credit score override a high ratio?

Creditworthiness is part of the wider assessment, but a strong score is not a universal exemption from debt-service or product requirements. Ask the lender what policy applies to your actual application.
