A thirty-year mortgage amortization can lower the scheduled payment compared with twenty-five years, but it usually leaves more principal outstanding and increases interest if you follow the longer schedule. The right comparison includes eligibility, mortgage insurance, the payment during your first term, the balance at renewal, and what you will do with the monthly difference.

In Canada, amortization is different from the mortgage term. You might select a five-year term with payments calculated over twenty-five or thirty years, then renew the remaining balance later. This guide compares those choices with an original calculation, using Canadian dollars and hypothetical interest rates. Eligibility and premium information were checked on November 14, 2026.

## Who can get a thirty-year amortization in Canada?

For insured high-ratio borrowing, current federal rules allow up to thirty years when at least one borrower is a first-time buyer or the property is newly built, subject to the other eligibility requirements. This is an either-or condition. You do not necessarily have to be both a first-time buyer and purchasing a newly built property.

Other insured buyers generally face a twenty-five-year maximum. For conventional borrowing with a larger down payment, the lender's available products and underwriting determine the options. Having 20% down does not oblige a lender to offer every amortization. Ask for confirmation tied to your application, property and proposed loan rather than treating an online selection box as eligibility.

[CMHC Home Start guidance](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/professionals/project-funding-and-mortgage-financing/mortgage-loan-insurance/mortgage-loan-insurance-homeownership-programs/home-start) describes the relevant insured product and its premiums. Confirm the definition of a first-time buyer and required evidence with the lender. Do not assume eligibility for one housing or tax program automatically establishes eligibility for another. The administrative definitions and documents can differ even when the everyday language sounds similar.

## Keep the mortgage amount and rate consistent

A fair first comparison holds the financed principal, payment frequency and rate constant. Otherwise a lower payment might result from a different loan amount or rate rather than the extra five years. Start with a clean calculation, then add product-specific insurance and pricing differences in a second comparison using actual lender quotes.

Our first example uses $400,000 financed, a hypothetical 4.5% fixed nominal annual rate compounded semi-annually, and monthly payments. There is no borrower-paid default-insurance premium in this initial comparison. The calculation is independent of a particular purchase price or lender. Values are rounded for display; underlying calculations retain full precision.

The mortgage payment formula uses the effective monthly rate, calculated as (1 + annual nominal rate / 2) raised to the power of one-sixth, minus one. It then spreads repayment across 300 or 360 monthly instalments. Using annual rate divided by twelve instead produces slightly different figures and makes comparisons with Canadian fixed-rate examples harder to reconcile.

## Payment, renewal balance and interest comparison

| Measure on the same $400,000 balance | 25 years | 30 years |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly payment at 4.5% | $2,213.89 | $2,016.86 |
| Balance after 60 payments | $351,186 | $364,401 |
| Interest over full schedule at unchanged rate | $264,168 | $326,070 |

The thirty-year schedule lowers the initial payment by approximately $197.03 monthly. Over the first five years that frees roughly $11,822 of cash flow. However, it also leaves approximately $13,216 more principal outstanding at that point. The difference reflects both slower repayment and the additional interest incurred on a higher remaining balance.

The lifetime interest difference is approximately $61,903 if the rate stays at 4.5% for the entire amortization and you make only the scheduled payments. That assumption is useful for isolating the schedule's effect, but it is not a prediction. Canadian mortgages commonly renew at different rates, so actual lifetime interest cannot be known from today's quote.

## Why the first renewal is a useful checkpoint

Many buyers expect to move or renegotiate before the final payment decades away. Compare the balance after your expected ownership period alongside lifetime projections. Selling after five years does not erase the slower principal repayment: the remaining mortgage generally has to be settled from the sale proceeds, subject to your agreement and transaction arrangements.

In the example, thirty years produces a lower payment today but a larger balance to renew. At the end of a five-year term, the original schedules would have approximately twenty and twenty-five years remaining respectively. A proper renewal calculation uses those remaining periods, not a fresh twenty-five or thirty years unless an extension is actually approved.

Ask the lender for an amortization schedule and keep it with your documents. At each annual review, compare the actual balance with the expected schedule. Extra payments, payment changes, fees, and changes in rate can alter the path. Monitoring the balance helps distinguish a temporary cash-flow choice from a longer-term increase in borrowing cost.

## Mortgage insurance changes the insured comparison

CMHC applies an additional 0.20 percentage-point premium surcharge to amortizations beyond twenty-five years. The surcharge is applied to the relevant loan amount; it is not 0.20% added to your annual mortgage interest rate. When the premium is financed, it also increases the balance on which mortgage interest is calculated.

Consider an eligible $500,000 purchase with $25,000 down. Before insurance, the mortgage is $475,000. At a standard 4% premium, the twenty-five-year financed balance would be $494,000. At 4.2%, the eligible thirty-year version would start at $494,950. The additional $950 must be included when calculating the longer schedule's payment and renewal balance.

The two insured payments therefore should not be calculated on identical balances. Use the actual premium and product quote for each option, and keep any applicable tax on the insurance premium in the closing-cash worksheet. Ask for a complete cash-to-close statement rather than assuming every cost can be rolled into the mortgage.

## Can thirty years increase mortgage affordability?

A longer eligible amortization lowers the payment used in a qualifying calculation on the same balance. At a hypothetical 6.5% qualifying rate, $400,000 requires approximately $2,679 monthly over twenty-five years or $2,506 over thirty. That difference may help debt-service ratios, depending on which constraint is limiting the application.

However, borrowing more can absorb all the initial payment relief. Compare two decisions separately: using thirty years for the same home, and using it to buy a more expensive home. The first may create room for savings; the second may leave the monthly budget equally tight while increasing the outstanding debt and exposure to future renewals.

Also check the cash limit. A longer schedule does not supply the down payment or closing funds. If your bottleneck is upfront cash, extending amortization may change little. The [mortgage affordability calculator](/mortgage-affordability-calculator/) and home cash planner address different pieces of the purchase; reconcile both before concluding that the longer schedule makes the transaction feasible.

## Decide what the monthly difference will accomplish

The example releases about $197 a month initially. Give that amount a specific job before selecting the longer option. It might rebuild an emergency reserve, cover a predictable period of parental leave, or eliminate expensive debt. The useful comparison is the resulting household plan, including risks and interest, rather than the mortgage payment in isolation.

Suppose an invented household has only $3,000 left after closing and essential spending of $4,500 monthly. A payment reduction might help rebuild its reserve, but it would take time. Another household with stable savings and ample surplus may prefer faster principal repayment. The same mortgage arithmetic can reasonably produce different decisions because the starting cash positions differ.

Set a review date and measurable target. For example, decide how much reserve to rebuild before directing available surplus toward principal. Revisit the plan when income changes or an expensive debt ends. Without an explicit destination for the difference, lower required payments can quietly turn into higher discretionary spending rather than improved resilience.

## Can you choose thirty years and pay like twenty-five?

Potentially, if your contract permits sufficient payment increases or lump-sum prepayments without a penalty. On the same principal and rate, consistently paying the twenty-five-year amount can reproduce the shorter mathematical repayment path. Actual product terms, insured balances and rate differences may prevent the two choices from being economically identical.

Read the prepayment provisions before relying on this strategy. Ask about annual limits, minimum lump sums, permitted payment increases, timing, and whether increased payments can later be reduced. A feature that allows extra repayment is different from an obligation to maintain a permanently higher scheduled payment. Get the explanation in writing with the mortgage commitment.

Behaviour matters too. An optional extra payment only works when you make it. Consider automating a permitted increase after your reserve reaches its target. If flexibility is the reason for selecting thirty years, document the conditions under which you will use that flexibility and when you intend to accelerate repayment again.

## Compare offers beyond the headline payment

Request parallel quotes for the same property, down payment, term and payment frequency. Record rate, financed balance, insurance premium, payment, expected balance at term-end, prepayment rights, portability, fees, and the method for calculating a break penalty. A small rate difference or expensive restriction can change the value of an apparently flexible longer schedule.

Include your expected moving timeline and employment uncertainty. A borrower likely to sell or refinance soon should investigate the relevant contract costs. A borrower approaching retirement should examine the balance likely to remain when earnings change. Do not assume a future refinance or an investment return will automatically repair a repayment plan that is uncomfortable today.

Write down why you selected the option. A useful decision record names the affordability constraint, what the cash-flow relief will fund, the reserve target, and the next review date. That record makes later changes deliberate. It also gives an adviser concrete assumptions to challenge rather than a vague preference for the lowest payment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a thirty-year mortgage term available?

This article compares amortizations, the estimated repayment period. The term is the length of your current mortgage agreement before renewal or payout. A thirty-year amortization does not mean your present interest rate is guaranteed for thirty years. Confirm both figures on every quote so a payment projection is not mistaken for a rate guarantee.

### Does everyone with less than 20% down qualify for thirty years?

No. The expanded insured option requires first-time-buyer or new-build eligibility along with the other program and lender conditions. Buyers outside that category generally remain subject to the shorter insured limit. The lender must also assess income, debts, credit, down-payment evidence and the property before approving the application.

### Which option builds equity faster?

With the same initial balance and rate, the twenty-five-year schedule repays principal faster. Equity also depends on property value and selling costs, which are not forecast here. Track the outstanding mortgage separately from market-price assumptions. A price increase is uncertain, while your contractual payment schedule can be examined today.

### What is the most useful next step?

Run both schedules using your actual expected balance, then compare the first-term cash flow and renewal balance. Add insurance differences and test a higher renewal rate. Bring the worksheet and household budget to a mortgage professional, and ask which products support the repayment flexibility you intend to use.
