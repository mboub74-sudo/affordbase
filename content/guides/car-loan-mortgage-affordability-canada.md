A car loan can reduce how much mortgage you qualify for in Canada because its required payment enters the total-debt calculation. The effect depends on your income, other obligations, housing expenses, qualifying interest rate and which debt-service limit is already binding. There is no reliable rule that every $500 vehicle payment removes the same mortgage amount for every borrower.

The useful approach is to compare the application with and without the payment while keeping every other assumption consistent. Then consider whether paying off the vehicle would leave enough for the down payment, closing costs and reserves. This guide uses original hypothetical Canadian-dollar scenarios; it is not a recommendation to repay a particular loan. Rules were checked on November 14, 2026.

## Why the monthly payment matters to mortgage qualification

A mortgage assessment examines both the housing commitment and the household's wider debt obligations. [CMHC total debt service framework](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/professionals/project-funding-and-mortgage-financing/mortgage-loan-insurance/calculating-gds-tds) includes other debts such as car loans, while the separate housing test excludes those non-housing payments. This means the vehicle payment competes for room under the total-debt ceiling rather than simply being subtracted from the home's price.

The amount still owed on the car and the required monthly payment describe different things. The balance matters when estimating the cash needed to eliminate the loan. The contractual payment matters when measuring the recurring obligation. Two borrowers with the same remaining vehicle balance can have different payments and therefore different debt-service results.

Record the actual payment frequency. A $300 biweekly payment averages $650 monthly because there are twenty-six biweekly periods in a year. Multiplying by two would show only $600 and understate the annual obligation by $600. Check the loan statement and convert the payment consistently with the lender's requested application format.

## Find the constraint before estimating the impact

For an illustration, assume accepted gross household income of $100,000 annually, $350 monthly property tax, $100 heating and no condo fee. Use benchmark housing and total-debt allowances of 39% and 44%. The monthly housing allowance is $3,250 and the total-debt allowance is $3,666.67. No other non-housing debt appears in this example.

With no vehicle payment, the housing allowance is the tighter constraint. The difference between the two allowances is approximately $416.67. A car payment below that gap may fit under the total-debt ceiling without reducing this particular maximum mortgage. It still consumes household cash and remains relevant to the full application and spending plan.

Once other debt payments exceed the gap, the total-debt test becomes tighter. Then each additional dollar of required monthly debt reduces the housing allowance by a dollar under these assumptions. This explains why a simple claim that any car payment always removes a fixed amount of mortgage room can be misleading.

## Worked example: four vehicle-payment scenarios

Use a hypothetical 4.5% contract rate and 6.5% qualifying rate, twenty-five-year amortization, monthly payments, and Canadian fixed-rate semi-annual compounding. At the qualifying rate, each $100,000 financed requires approximately $669.82 monthly. The table shows financed mortgage capacity, including any premium that must fit within the loan; purchase price is a separate calculation.

| Monthly car payment | Housing allowance after both debt tests | Mortgage payment room after $450 tax and heat | Supported financed mortgage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0 | $3,250.00 | $2,800.00 | $418,020 |
| $300 | $3,250.00 | $2,800.00 | $418,020 |
| $600 | $3,066.67 | $2,616.67 | $390,650 |
| $900 | $2,766.67 | $2,316.67 | $345,862 |

Moving from no car payment to $600 reduces supported principal by about $27,370 in this example. Moving from $600 to $900 reduces it by approximately $44,788. The unequal effects come from the initial gap between the housing and total-debt allowances. They do not mean a lender values one car differently; the binding constraint changed.

When the total-debt limit is already binding, an additional $100 monthly obligation removes approximately $14,929 of supported principal at this example's rate and amortization. Change the qualifying rate, schedule or debt limits and that conversion changes. Keep this number attached to its assumptions rather than presenting it as a national mortgage rule.

## Should you pay off the car before buying a house?

Compare complete plans. Paying off a vehicle may remove a monthly obligation but also reduce available cash. A better debt-service result is useful only if the remaining down payment, closing funds and reserve still support the intended purchase. Ask the lender to calculate both versions using the actual payout amount and the cash source.

Imagine you have $80,000 in accessible savings and a $15,000 vehicle payout. Repaying it leaves $65,000. If the purchase plan requires $55,000 for down payment, $12,000 for closing and $10,000 of retained reserve, it needs $77,000 total. The payoff would improve recurring cash flow but create an invented $12,000 funding gap under that plan.

You could revise the purchase, timing or cash allocation, but do not ignore the gap. The alternative of keeping the loan also requires qualification and a workable household budget. The choice should compare borrowing eligibility, interest saved, cash remaining and ongoing affordability. A single maximum-price result cannot decide all four issues.

## Partial repayment may not remove the required payment

A lump-sum payment can reduce a balance or shorten repayment without changing the contractual monthly obligation. The effect depends on the loan agreement and servicing arrangement. If your goal is mortgage qualification, ask whether the lender will recognize a lower required payment after partial repayment and what documentary evidence it requires.

For example, using $5,000 of savings to reduce a car balance might save interest while leaving the same $600 required payment. In that case the debt-service worksheet may not gain the room you expected. It can still be a valid financial choice, but its benefit should not be confused with eliminating a monthly obligation.

Obtain a current payout statement before planning a full repayment. Confirm any fees, timing, final payment process and proof that the debt has been discharged. Ask the mortgage lender when that evidence must be available. An anticipated balance reduction is different from a completed transaction that underwriting has accepted in the application.

## Refinancing the car creates a different trade-off

Extending the vehicle loan might lower its required payment, but a longer schedule can increase interest and leave the debt outstanding for more years. Compare total remaining payments, rate, fees, term and expected vehicle value. A lower payment alone does not establish that refinancing improves the household's overall financial position.

It can also create a new credit application during a sensitive period of the mortgage process. Discuss any proposed change with the mortgage professional before entering an agreement. The new loan's terms, credit inquiry and reporting timeline may matter. Do not rely on a salesperson's estimate of mortgage benefits without a lender-specific assessment.

Keep the vehicle decision grounded in transportation needs. A reliable car may support employment, while an expensive replacement can consume both cash and borrowing capacity. Compare keeping the existing vehicle, reducing the target vehicle cost, postponing a purchase, and other practical options. Include likely repairs and operating costs rather than assuming the cheapest payment means the cheapest transport.

## A lease still belongs in the conversation

Leasing a vehicle does not make its payment disappear from the household's obligations. Disclose the lease and ask how the mortgage lender will treat it, including any relevant end-of-lease commitments. The applicable assessment depends on the lender and file. Treat the advertised monthly lease amount as the beginning of your review rather than the complete cost.

For your own budget, include insurance, fuel or charging, maintenance, parking, registration and any expected lease-end charges. These operating expenses may not all enter the formal debt-service ratio, but they still reduce money available for housing. A household can pass the mortgage debt tests while facing a tight combined housing-and-transportation budget.

Compare location choices too. A lower-priced home farther from work might require another car or materially higher commuting costs. Estimate that change before increasing the offer budget. The additional monthly transportation expense can offset part of the mortgage saving even if the formal qualifying worksheet does not capture every operating cost.

## Account for other debts in the same application

The table isolates the car payment, but real households often have several obligations. Add the required treatment of credit cards, student loans, lines of credit and other commitments. Ask the lender which payment amount it uses rather than entering only what you voluntarily paid last month. Different debt types can require different calculation methods.

Suppose another $300 monthly obligation already exists. Adding a $300 car payment now creates $600 total non-housing debt, so the table's $600 row becomes the relevant comparison. Looking only at the car in isolation would overstate remaining room. Keep a complete list with balances, required payments, frequencies and expected end dates.

Do not remove an obligation because you expect it to end soon without checking the lender's policy. Likewise, avoid hiding a payment that is not visible on the first credit report you inspect. Clear documentation allows the lender to assess the real situation and tells you which change would actually improve the application.

## Protect the application between pre-approval and closing

Before financing a vehicle, ask the mortgage professional to rerun the application. Supply the proposed payment, loan amount, term, rate and down payment rather than just the car's sticker price. A preliminary home-buying estimate may change when new obligations appear. Make the trade-off concrete before committing to either transaction.

After any approved debt change, keep statements and confirmation documents. Update the household budget and verify that the mortgage lender has received what it needs. If the vehicle is essential before the home purchase, adjust the property target to the revised financing and cash position instead of assuming the old pre-approval remains an unchanged ceiling.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a car loan automatically prevent mortgage approval?

No. Many borrowers can carry both obligations. The lender assesses the combination of income, debts, property, credit and available cash. This guide's $300 payment example leaves the same mathematical mortgage capacity because the housing limit remains tighter. That example is not a promise that a particular application will be approved.

### Will paying the car off increase my maximum purchase price?

It may improve debt-service room, but using savings for the payoff reduces cash available toward the home. Recalculate the down payment, default insurance if applicable, closing expenses and reserves. Ask for a side-by-side application assessment rather than assuming the mortgage increase automatically exceeds the cash used to eliminate the loan.

### Is a smaller car payment always better?

A smaller required payment can help monthly cash flow and debt-service calculations, but extending the debt may increase total interest or other costs. Compare the full agreement and your expected vehicle needs. The most suitable arrangement must fit both the transportation plan and the home-buying timeline.

### What should I calculate next?

Enter all debts in the [mortgage affordability calculator](/mortgage-affordability-calculator/), verify the proposed mortgage payment, and build a combined housing-and-transportation budget from take-home pay. Compare keeping and repaying the car loan with the same property assumptions. Bring both worksheets and a current payout statement to the mortgage professional for an application-specific assessment.
