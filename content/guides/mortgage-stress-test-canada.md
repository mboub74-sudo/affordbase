The mortgage stress test in Canada checks whether you can carry a mortgage at a qualifying rate above the rate in your offer. For a standard new mortgage subject to the test, that rate is the greater of your contract rate plus two percentage points and 5.25%. Passing depends on the payment calculated at that rate, your accepted income, property expenses, other debts, and the lender's complete assessment.

The practical question is how much room remains after those inputs interact. This guide turns the test into a worksheet, compares contract and qualifying payments, and shows how to investigate an unexpected calculator result. Rules were checked on November 14, 2026. All examples use Canadian dollars and hypothetical rates; they are planning calculations rather than rate quotes or lending decisions.

## What the Canadian mortgage stress test actually measures

Your contract rate determines the scheduled interest calculation during the mortgage term. Your qualifying rate is used to assess borrowing capacity. These rates perform different jobs. A lender does not normally charge the stress-test payment simply because it used that payment when evaluating a new application. Your agreement determines what you actually owe.

For example, a hypothetical 4.5% contract rate produces a 6.5% qualifying rate under the standard rule. A 3% contract rate instead produces 5.25%, because adding two percentage points gives only 5%. Calculate both alternatives and use the higher one. Adding 2% of the rate itself would be the wrong calculation.

[OSFI publishes the minimum qualifying rate](https://www.osfi-bsif.gc.ca/en/supervision/financial-institutions/banks/minimum-qualifying-rate-uninsured-mortgages) for uninsured mortgages at federally regulated lenders. Insured mortgages also have qualification requirements through the applicable insurance framework. A credit union or another lender may operate under different requirements or policies. Ask which framework applies before assuming every institution must produce the same borrowing limit.

## Start with an evidence-based input sheet

Collect annual income that the lender will accept, rather than simply multiplying your best recent paycheque. Record the applicants included, income type, employment history, and documents requested. A second applicant can add accepted earnings, but their debts also enter the assessment. A household's bank deposits are not automatically the income figure used for qualification.

Next record the intended down payment, property tax, heating estimate, condominium fee if applicable, and monthly payments on other obligations. Keep monthly and annual amounts in separate columns. Dividing annual property tax by twelve once is correct; dividing a figure already entered monthly would understate the expense and inflate apparent borrowing power.

Include the full financed mortgage balance. If mortgage default insurance is added to the loan, the payment calculation needs to include that premium. Confirm the amortization you are eligible for and the payment frequency. A worksheet that ignores insurance or silently assumes thirty years may look reassuring while describing financing you cannot actually obtain.

## Calculate the housing allowance before the mortgage amount

For this illustration, use the CMHC maximum debt-service benchmarks: 39% for gross debt service and 44% for total debt service. These are inputs to an example, not universal promises from every lender. The housing test and the overall debt test must both work. The smaller available allowance controls the next step.

Suppose accepted gross household income is $100,000 annually, or $8,333.33 monthly. A 39% housing allowance is $3,250. A 44% total-debt allowance is $3,666.67. With $300 in other qualifying monthly debt payments, the latter leaves $3,366.67 for housing. The $3,250 housing allowance is therefore the tighter constraint.

Now subtract $350 monthly property tax and $150 heating, with no condo fee. That leaves $2,750 for the qualifying principal-and-interest payment. This is the amount to convert into mortgage principal. Treating $3,250 as available entirely for the mortgage would forget $500 of property expenses and substantially overstate the result.

## Worked example: contract payment versus qualifying payment

The following calculation assumes a Canadian fixed-rate convention: nominal annual interest compounded semi-annually, monthly payments, and a twenty-five-year amortization. The mortgage balance includes any amount being financed. It holds property expenses constant so you can see the effect of the rate alone. Figures are rounded after calculation.

| Calculation | Hypothetical contract rate | Qualifying rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual nominal rate | 4.5% | 6.5% |
| Monthly payment per $100,000 | $553.47 | $669.82 |
| Principal supported by $2,750 monthly | $496,863 | $410,556 |

Using only the contract payment would suggest approximately $86,307 more mortgage principal than the qualifying calculation supports. This difference is not a prediction of future interest rates. It shows why an advertised payment and a borrowing-capacity estimate answer different questions, even when both calculations are mathematically correct.

A $400,000 mortgage illustrates the same distinction more directly: the contract payment is about $2,214, while the qualifying payment is about $2,679. Those amounts exclude tax and heat. Add the stated $500 property expenses to each if comparing total housing costs. Do not compare an all-in number with a mortgage-only number.

## Change one assumption at a time

Rate sensitivity is useful when negotiating price or assessing another property. Keeping the $2,750 payment allowance and twenty-five-year schedule unchanged, a 5.5% qualifying rate supports about $450,531, while a 7.5% qualifying rate supports about $375,912. These are financed balances, not purchase prices. They also assume the same compounding convention throughout.

Higher property costs reduce the allowance before any principal is calculated. An additional $200 monthly expense leaves $2,550 instead of $2,750. At the example's 6.5% qualifying rate, that change removes roughly $29,859 of supported mortgage principal. Check actual property documents before relying on a generic tax or heating estimate.

Other debts affect the total-debt allowance. At $700 monthly debt instead of $300, the example's total-debt test leaves $2,966.67 for housing, below the $3,250 housing limit. After tax and heating, $2,466.67 remains for the qualifying mortgage payment. Improving one input helps only until a different constraint becomes the limiting factor.

## Convert mortgage capacity into a realistic purchase budget

Mortgage capacity is the maximum financed balance under stated assumptions. Purchase price must also account for the down payment and any financed default-insurance premium. Simply adding your cash to the maximum mortgage can be wrong when the premium consumes part of that borrowing capacity. Work from a candidate price and recalculate the actual required loan.

Separate the down payment from closing cash. Legal services, transfer taxes where applicable, adjustments, inspections, moving expenses, and a reserve can all require money outside the mortgage. A buyer with $60,000 in savings may not have $60,000 available to contribute toward the purchase price after those obligations are funded.

Run the candidate property through the [mortgage affordability calculator](/mortgage-affordability-calculator/), then verify the payment in the mortgage payment calculator. Use the home cash planner for upfront expenses. If the three outputs do not reconcile, inspect the assumptions rather than averaging the results. Every dollar should have one role and appear once in the appropriate worksheet.

## Passing the test does not complete your household budget

Imagine take-home pay of $6,200, a contract mortgage payment of $2,214, and the example's $500 tax-and-heating allowance. Add $650 for insurance, other utilities and maintenance savings, leaving $2,836 before food, transportation, debt, childcare, savings and personal expenses. These invented figures show the next calculation; they are not recommended spending targets.

Build your version from actual payroll deposits and recent bills. Include irregular expenses through monthly reserves. Then rehearse a higher payment, one applicant taking unpaid leave, or a costly repair. The purpose is to identify the purchase price you can carry through ordinary setbacks, even if the lender would permit a larger loan.

An informative test has a response attached. If the higher-payment scenario leaves a deficit, decide whether to lower the target price, delay the purchase, reduce another commitment, or increase the reserve. A spreadsheet that displays a red number without changing the buying plan has identified a problem but has not solved it.

## What to do if the estimate is too low

Ask which constraint is binding: accepted income, debt payments, housing expenses, down payment, insurance eligibility, credit, or property suitability. This diagnosis determines the useful next step. Saving another $5,000 may help a cash shortage, while eliminating a required monthly payment may matter more when total debt is the main obstacle.

Compare documented lender quotes on equivalent assumptions. A lower contract rate may lower the qualifying rate, but the 5.25% floor can limit that effect. A longer eligible amortization may reduce the qualifying payment while increasing interest exposure. Inspect both the immediate borrowing impact and the ongoing cost before treating a bigger approval as a better result.

Avoid changing credit commitments between pre-approval and closing without checking the consequences. A new vehicle loan or additional balance can alter the file. Report material changes promptly and ask what supporting evidence is needed. A preliminary borrowing estimate does not remove the lender's need to assess the final application and property.

## Renewal and switching rules need a separate check

Do not assume that every renewal or lender change follows a new-purchase calculation. OSFI no longer prescribes its minimum qualifying rate for qualifying uninsured straight switches at renewal between federally regulated institutions, subject to the stated conditions. Increasing the loan or extending the remaining amortization can change how the transaction is treated.

That exception does not force a new lender to approve you. Credit, income and prudent underwriting still matter. Ask whether your proposed transfer satisfies the precise straight-switch requirements and whether any new borrowing is involved. Keep a renewal comparison separate from a purchase example so an exception is not accidentally applied to the wrong transaction.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does putting 20% down remove the stress test?

No. Avoiding borrower-paid default insurance does not automatically remove standard uninsured mortgage qualification at a federally regulated lender. A larger down payment can lower the loan required, but the lender still assesses the application under the applicable rules and its own policies. Ask about the specific mortgage product rather than relying on a down-payment shortcut.

### Is the qualifying rate the interest rate I will pay?

Usually it is an assessment input, while your contract determines the scheduled rate and payments. Compare the commitment's actual terms with the worksheet. A variable-rate agreement or later renewal can change future payments, so distinguish today's contractual obligation from a hypothetical qualification amount and from a forecast of future costs.

### Why do mortgage calculators show different answers?

They may use different debt-service limits, property expenses, insurance assumptions, compounding conventions, or amortizations. Some estimate a comfortable budget while others estimate a lender limit. Save each input screen and compare the settings line by line. A difference becomes useful once you can identify the assumption producing it.

### What should I take to a mortgage appointment?

Bring your income evidence, a complete debt-payment list, down-payment records, and a candidate property's tax, fee and heating information. Include your household spending worksheet and the assumptions behind any online estimate. Request a written explanation of the borrowing limit, remaining conditions, and the payment you would actually make.
