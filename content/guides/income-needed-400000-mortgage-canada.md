# Income Needed for a $400,000 Mortgage in Canada

The income needed for a $400,000 mortgage in Canada depends on the qualifying payment, property taxes, heating, condominium fees, other debts, and the income a lender accepts. Under the specific example in this guide, a household with no other debt needs approximately $97,825 of gross annual income to satisfy two selected debt-ratio benchmarks. That is a calculated scenario, not a universal lending threshold.

The example assumes a $400,000 total mortgage balance, a hypothetical 4.5% contract rate, a 6.5% qualifying rate, twenty-five-year amortization, $400 monthly property taxes, and $100 monthly heat. All amounts are Canadian dollars. Changing these inputs changes the answer, and meeting the ratios does not establish approval or prove that the complete household budget is comfortable.

## Define the $400,000 correctly

A $400,000 mortgage is not necessarily a $400,000 purchase. A buyer could purchase a $500,000 home with a $100,000 down payment and borrow $400,000 before any applicable additions. Another buyer could have a different purchase price and equity contribution. The mortgage balance, purchase price, and cash contribution must remain separate throughout the calculation.

Also specify whether $400,000 includes a financed mortgage insurance premium. If you calculate payments on a base loan and later add the premium, the payment and required income rise. For the main example here, $400,000 means the full balance being amortized. It does not mean a base amount to which uncounted financing costs will be added afterward.

Use the lender's actual advance and financing details when reviewing an offer. Purchase adjustments, closing expenses, and the mortgage's face amount do not always translate into the same net funds available on closing day. A clear funding statement prevents the down payment or loan amount from being inferred incorrectly from a single payment quote.

## Calculate the qualifying mortgage payment

For a new mortgage subject to the standard Canadian stress test, the qualifying rate is the greater of the contract rate plus two percentage points and 5.25%. A hypothetical 4.5% contract rate therefore produces a 6.5% qualifying rate. OSFI specifies this framework for most newly underwritten uninsured residential mortgages at federally regulated lenders; insured qualification also has applicable stress-test requirements.

At 6.5% over twenty-five years, a $400,000 balance produces approximately $2,679.30 in monthly principal and interest using nominal semi-annual compounding. This is the qualification payment in the example. At the hypothetical 4.5% contract rate under the same convention, the payment is approximately $2,213.89. The difference is about $465.41 per month.

The assessment payment does not mean the lender charges 6.5% when your contract says 4.5%. It tests a higher-payment scenario. Conversely, using only the lower contract payment in a qualification worksheet can overstate the amount of income available to support the loan. Keep both figures labelled so the approval analysis and spending budget remain distinct.

## Add property costs before applying a ratio

The main example adds $400 a month in property taxes and $100 for heat. Together with $2,679.30 principal and interest, qualifying housing costs are approximately $3,179.30 a month. These property figures are invented inputs. They are not averages for Canada or estimates associated with every home carrying a $400,000 mortgage.

For a condominium, applicable calculations generally include 50% of the condo fee. A $500 monthly fee would therefore add $250 to this qualification example, increasing its housing total to approximately $3,429.30. Your personal spending plan must still cover the entire $500 fee. The qualification treatment does not reduce the amount you owe the condominium corporation.

Use the relevant property's tax information and credible heating records or estimates. A lower purchase price does not automatically imply lower recurring costs, and a mortgage balance alone says little about the property's maintenance needs. Ask how a lender handles unusual tenure, site rent, or other property-specific charges before assuming the basic worksheet is complete.

## Work backward from GDS

For this comparison, use a 39% gross debt service benchmark. Divide monthly qualifying housing costs by 0.39 to estimate the minimum gross monthly income under that test. Using the unrounded payment, $3,179.295 of housing costs divided by 0.39 is approximately $8,152.04. Multiplying by twelve produces about $97,824.47 of annual gross income.

Rounded upward for a simple planning figure, that is approximately $97,825 a year. Rounding is useful for readability, but an application near a boundary needs the lender's exact inputs and rules. A small difference in heating, tax, payment convention, or accepted income can move a borderline result. Avoid presenting the rounded number as a guaranteed cutoff.

This calculation uses gross income because that is the ratio's denominator. It does not imply that $8,152.04 is available for spending each month. Payroll deductions and other household needs still apply. After running the qualification worksheet, repeat the ownership budget using actual take-home income and every recurring expense.

## Run the total-debt test separately

Using a 44% total debt service benchmark, add other qualifying debt payments to the housing total and divide by 0.44. With no other debt, the example requires approximately $86,708.05 annually under TDS. Since GDS requires approximately $97,824.47, GDS is the tighter test and determines the larger income figure.

With $300 of other qualifying monthly debt, TDS requires approximately $94,889.87 annually. GDS remains tighter under these particular assumptions. With $600 of other debt, however, TDS requires approximately $103,071.69 a year. The required planning income becomes approximately $103,072 because both tests must be satisfied, not averaged together.

Use qualifying debt payments rather than guessing from balances. A vehicle loan, revolving credit, student debt, or another obligation may be treated differently depending on the agreement and underwriting rules. Ask the lender to identify the amount used for each line. Your bank statement's current minimum is not necessarily the qualification amount.

## See what condo fees and higher taxes change

Add the fictional $500 condo fee discussed earlier, with half counted for qualification. At the same rate, amortization, tax, and heat assumptions, the GDS-based income rises to approximately $105,516.77 annually. That is about $7,692 more than the no-condo-fee example. The mortgage principal has not changed; the added qualifying housing cost creates the difference.

Likewise, an additional $100 monthly property-tax allowance raises the GDS-based annual income requirement by approximately $3,076.92, calculated as $100 divided by 0.39 and multiplied by twelve. If another ratio is already tighter, the final effect depends on that competing constraint. Change one input at a time to understand what drives the result.

For an actual comparison, evaluate complete properties rather than hypothetical identical homes with one changed bill. One property may have lower taxes but higher fees or maintenance exposure. Use the qualification worksheet to identify borrowing constraints and a separate ownership-cost worksheet to compare the full financial commitment.

## Understand the effect of amortization

A longer amortization spreads scheduled principal repayment over more time, lowering the payment for the same balance and rate. In the purely mathematical example, thirty years at the same 6.5% qualifying rate produces approximately $2,505.61 monthly principal and interest. Adding $500 for tax and heat implies approximately $92,480.30 annual income under the 39% GDS test.

That comparison does not establish eligibility for a thirty-year product or identical pricing. Insured thirty-year eligibility has specific conditions, and premiums or other terms may differ. Ask the lender what product you can actually use before treating the smaller calculated payment as an available option. Recalculate with all required charges included.

Also compare total interest and the balance remaining at the end of the mortgage term. A lower scheduled payment can improve immediate cash flow while repaying principal more slowly. The appropriate amortization depends on affordability, eligibility, long-term cost, and the flexibility you want, not solely the lowest qualifying income shown by a calculator.

## Check the cash required beyond the loan

If a $500,000 purchase with 20% down creates the $400,000 mortgage, the $100,000 down payment is only one funding requirement. Closing expenses, adjustments, moving costs, and money retained after purchase need their own plan. A household can satisfy income ratios while lacking the funds required to complete the transaction safely.

Confirm the source and availability of the down payment. Money expected from an asset sale, gift, or account withdrawal may require documentation and processing time. A lender may assess borrowed funds differently from nonrepayable funds. Keep the financing assumptions aligned with the actual source rather than assuming every dollar of apparent savings has the same treatment.

When comparing lenders, include fees and conditions alongside the rate. The lowest headline rate may not be the most useful offer if its restrictions conflict with your expected plans. Review payment privileges, term, portability, and the cost implications of a possible early exit using the actual contract information supplied.

## Make the income target useful for your household

Build a monthly ownership budget with the contract payment, full taxes, utilities, insurance, maintenance, and any condo fee. Add food, transport, childcare, health needs, debt payments, and savings. Compare the total with dependable take-home income. The qualification calculation deliberately leaves out several expenses that can strongly affect daily comfort.

Test an income interruption and a higher-cost month. A household with two incomes may still face shared employment risks or a planned leave. Someone with irregular earnings may need more cash between deposits. Use these practical constraints to choose a borrowing amount, even if the lender would consider a larger one.

Prepare an application summary showing accepted income assumptions, proposed principal, amortization, qualifying rate, property costs, and debts. Ask the lender or broker to confirm each input and identify any conditions. This turns a broad search question into a concrete assessment you can compare with the household's own comfort limit.

## Compare payment schedules on a consistent basis

A monthly payment quote cannot be compared directly with a biweekly or weekly quote without converting the schedule. There are twenty-six biweekly payments in a normal fifty-two-week year, compared with twelve monthly payments. Multiplying a biweekly figure by two understates the annual amount when presented as a monthly equivalent. Ask whether a quoted schedule is regular or accelerated and how that changes total annual payments.

Keep the qualification calculation consistent with the lender's method, then place actual payment dates into the household cash-flow calendar. A household can have enough annual income while still needing a cushion between paydays and withdrawal dates. Use a confirmed schedule rather than assuming every month contains the same number of deposits and mortgage debits.

When comparing offers, also keep the principal, term, amortization, compounding convention, and included charges aligned. A smaller-looking payment can result from a longer repayment period or an omitted fee. Explain the source of the difference before deciding that one offer requires less income or creates a lower overall commitment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is $100,000 enough income for a $400,000 mortgage?

It may fit some numerical scenarios and fail others. In this guide's no-debt example, the calculated requirement is approximately $97,825 gross annually. With $600 in other qualifying monthly debt, it rises to approximately $103,072. Different property costs, rates, products, and accepted income can change the outcome, and other underwriting conditions still apply.

### Can I use a simple income multiple instead?

A multiple can provide a rough conversation starter, but it cannot capture property taxes, debt payments, condo fees, financed premiums, or the qualifying rate. Use the full worksheet when deciding whether a specific mortgage fits. Two households with the same salary and loan balance can have materially different qualification results and spending capacity.

### What should I verify before making an offer?

Confirm the mortgage amount including financed additions, the accepted income, the property's costs, and the approval conditions. Check available closing funds and the complete after-tax budget as well. Use AffordBase's payment and stress-test calculators to organize your estimates, then obtain a lender assessment tied to the actual application and property before relying on the result.
