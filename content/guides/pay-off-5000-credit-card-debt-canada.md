To pay off $5,000 in credit card debt, choose a payment your budget can sustain, stop adding new charges to that balance, and track the result each statement cycle. A fixed payment can make progress easier to measure than following a shrinking minimum. However, it must always cover at least the minimum actually required by your issuer.

This Canadian guide uses an original repayment model to compare $200, $300, and $450 monthly payments. The examples are planning estimates, not a promise about your card. Actual interest calculations, fees, transaction types, payment dates, and provincial requirements can change the result. Begin with your statement and agreement rather than a generic online minimum-payment assumption.

## Gather the numbers before choosing a strategy

Write down the current balance, purchase interest rate, cash-advance rate if relevant, minimum due, and due date. Identify overdue amounts, annual fees, installment plans, and any promotional balances. A card can contain more than one rate, so a single headline percentage may not describe the entire amount owed.

Check whether an automatic payment already exists and what it pays: the minimum, a fixed amount, or the full statement balance. An old instruction may no longer match your plan. Make sure the funding account contains enough cash before the withdrawal and verify that an extra manual payment does not create an unexpected duplicate withdrawal.

Use a separate line for new spending. If you pay $300 but add $250 in purchases, the balance will not fall as if you had made a clean $300 debt payment. Interest can make the difference even larger. Separating repayment from current consumption is essential for understanding whether the plan is working.

## What $5,000 costs at different fixed payments

For this illustration, start with $5,000 at a constant 19.99% annual rate. Calculate interest monthly as the opening balance multiplied by 19.99% divided by twelve, then apply the payment at month-end. Assume no new purchases, fees, missed payments, rate changes, or promotional conditions. The last payment is smaller than the regular amount.

| Fixed monthly payment | Approximate payoff time | Modelled interest | Total repaid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $200 | 33 months | $1,521 | $6,521 |
| $300 | 20 months | $906 | $5,906 |
| $450 | 13 months | $576 | $5,576 |

The $300 plan saves approximately thirteen months and $615 in interest compared with the $200 plan in this model. Moving from $300 to $450 saves another seven months and about $330. Those comparisons describe the assumed schedule, not the exact statement calculation used by a particular bank.

Many issuers calculate interest using daily balances and actual transaction dates. Use the issuer's figures or the official FCAC calculator for a closer estimate, then reconcile your own statements. If the required minimum exceeds a table payment, pay the required amount or arrange assistance; the table does not override your agreement.

## Understand why the first payment feels slow

Under the model, first-month interest is approximately $83.29. A $300 payment therefore reduces principal by about $216.71, leaving approximately $4,783.29. That is still meaningful progress even though the balance did not fall by the full $300. The interest portion declines as the balance shrinks if the rate remains unchanged.

At $200, the first principal reduction is about $116.71. At $450, it is about $366.71. This explains why an additional payment can have a noticeable effect without requiring a dramatic change in the stated rate. More of each payment reaches principal once the interest due has been covered.

Track the statement balance, interest charged, fees, new purchases, and payment separately each month. If your observed result differs from the model, look for a factual explanation rather than assuming the bank made an error. A longer statement period, cash advance, new charge, or payment date can all change the calculation.

## Build a payment that fits your actual cash flow

Consider a fictional monthly take-home income of $3,600. Rent and utilities cost $1,450, food and household essentials $500, transport $300, insurance and phone $180, other required debt payments $170, irregular-bill reserves $200, and flexible spending $300. These categories total $3,100 and leave $500 unassigned.

The household could assign $300 to the card and $200 to a starter buffer. That choice sacrifices some repayment speed in exchange for cash to handle small disruptions without borrowing again. It is an example of allocating the available $500, not a universal recommendation that everyone keep the same reserve or payment.

Check the dates as well as the total. If rent is due before the next paycheck, a month-end surplus may not be available on the card's due date. The [biweekly paycheck budgeting guide](/articles/biweekly-paycheck-budget/) shows how to reserve money across pay periods. A good repayment target must survive the calendar, not just the monthly arithmetic.

## Make the minimum a floor, not the whole plan

Your statement's minimum is the amount required to keep the account current under its terms, not necessarily an efficient route to a zero balance. FCAC explains that paying more than the minimum generally shortens repayment and reduces interest. The issuer's formula and the applicable rules determine the amount actually due.

For Quebec residents, FCAC notes that the credit-card minimum payment has been 5% since August 1, 2025. Do not apply a generic 3% example to a Quebec account without checking the statement. On a simple $5,000 balance, 5% alone is $250, so a $200 plan may already be insufficient before other required amounts.

If using a fixed target, keep it at least as high as the current minimum and review each new statement. As the balance falls, maintaining the same affordable target can accelerate progress compared with lowering it automatically. When cash is tight, protect required payments first and contact the issuer before the due date about a shortfall.

## Stop the cycle of repayment followed by reborrowing

Move routine spending to a method you can fund from current income, while accounting for pending transactions and recurring subscriptions. Remove saved card details from shopping apps if easy access is a problem. These changes do not erase the debt, but they separate the old balance from this month's living expenses.

Do not make a payment so aggressive that groceries or a necessary bus pass must go back onto the card a week later. A sustainable $300 payment with no new borrowing may work better than an occasional $500 payment followed by repeated charges. Monitor the balance over several cycles rather than judging success by the largest payment made.

Set aside predictable costs such as annual insurance or school expenses in small amounts. They are not surprises merely because they are not monthly. The [sinking-funds guide](/articles/sinking-funds/) can help turn those bills into planned allocations, reducing the chance that a new obligation interrupts the repayment schedule.

## Compare balance transfers using complete costs

A balance-transfer promotion can reduce interest, but approval, available credit, transfer fees, promotional duration, and the later rate all matter. Read the agreement before applying. Check what happens after a late payment, whether purchases have separate terms, and how payments are allocated between balances carrying different rates.

Suppose a hypothetical offer charges a 3% transfer fee on $5,000 and offers twelve months at 0% on the transferred amount. The fee is $150. If financed with the transfer, clearing $5,150 within twelve months requires about $429.17 per month. A household able to pay only $300 would still owe money when the promotion ends.

That calculation is not a product recommendation or a claim that such an offer is available. It is a way to test the advertisement against your capacity. Compare the projected balance at the promotional expiry date and apply the actual post-promotion rate to the remaining amount before deciding whether the transfer helps.

## Evaluate consolidation without stretching debt indefinitely

A lower-rate loan may make repayment easier to organize, but compare the term, fees, total interest, and required payment. A smaller payment spread over many years is not automatically cheaper. Ask for the full repayment schedule and compare it with the same debt-free target date you would use for the card.

Consider what happens to the cleared credit limit. If the card is used again while the consolidation loan remains, total debt can rise rather than fall. Decide in advance how you will manage access to revolving credit and how everyday spending will be funded. The behavior around the new loan matters as much as its rate.

Avoid converting unsecured debt into debt secured on your home merely because the payment looks smaller without examining the consequences. Collateral, repayment length, legal costs, and the risk to the asset change the decision. Obtain a clear explanation of the actual agreement rather than treating every consolidation product as equivalent.

## Choose a method when you have several debts

List all balances, rates, required payments, and any overdue amounts. Keep required payments current across accounts, then decide where extra funds go. A highest-rate approach targets interest cost, while a smallest-balance approach may provide earlier account closures. The [snowball versus avalanche comparison](/articles/debt-snowball-vs-avalanche/) explains the trade-offs.

Past-due accounts or obligations with urgent consequences may need attention before a simple rate ranking. A repayment spreadsheet is not a substitute for understanding collection notices, essential services, or secured debts. If you are unsure which payment is urgent, seek qualified assistance and explain the complete situation rather than only the card balance.

Use one total extra-payment budget across the plan. Do not allocate the same $200 to several accounts on separate worksheets. Once an account is repaid, deliberately redirect its former payment rather than allowing the amount to disappear into spending. That keeps progress visible and avoids accidental double counting.

## Know when a self-directed plan is not enough

If your income cannot cover essentials and required debt payments, another budgeting tip may not close the gap. Contact the issuer early and ask what arrangements may be available. Request the proposed rate, payment, duration, fees, and credit-reporting implications in writing. Do not assume an advertised hardship option applies automatically.

FCAC's debt guidance describes avenues for help, including credit counselling. Before agreeing to a service, understand what it does, its fees, creditor participation, and the effect on your accounts. For formal insolvency questions, speak with a Licensed Insolvency Trustee rather than relying on a company promising a guaranteed government debt-erasure program.

Keep a record of conversations and continue checking statements. An application for help does not necessarily change the payment obligation until an arrangement is confirmed. A realistic next step may be stabilizing essentials and negotiating terms before targeting an ambitious payoff date. That is a different task from choosing between two affordable fixed payments.

## A simple monthly review

At each statement, compare opening balance plus interest, fees, and purchases minus payments with the closing balance. Note what caused any difference from the expected path. Adjust future estimates using actual figures while keeping the payment sustainable. This small reconciliation is more informative than checking only an app's progress graphic.

When the card is nearly cleared, confirm the payoff amount and look for residual interest on the next statement. Once the balance is settled, decide where the former debt payment goes. Redirecting it toward a reserve or another goal can preserve the improvement in cash flow instead of replacing the debt with new recurring spending.

## Frequently asked questions

### How fast can I repay $5,000 at $300 a month?

In the simplified 19.99% model above, it takes about twenty months with a smaller final payment. Your card may differ because of daily interest, fees, new purchases, and required minimums. Use actual statements to refine the estimate and keep every payment at least as high as the amount due.

### Should I use all my savings to pay the card?

Compare interest savings with the cash needed for essentials and likely disruptions. Emptying the account may cause immediate reborrowing. Identify a suitable reserve and known near-term bills, then decide how much is available. The right amount depends on your income reliability, obligations, and access to other support.

### Will paying off the card guarantee a higher credit score?

No exact score change can be guaranteed. Lower balances and reliable payments can improve your overall financial position, but scoring models and files differ. Focus on accurate records, affordable obligations, and avoiding missed payments. A promised number of points is not a useful basis for the repayment plan.

### Is a lower-rate transfer always worthwhile?

No. Include transfer fees, the expiry date, the required payoff payment, and the rate on any remaining balance. Also consider eligibility and the terms for new purchases. A promotion that cannot be cleared before expiry may still help, but its full cost needs calculating rather than assuming the headline rate applies forever.
