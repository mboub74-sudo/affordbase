# What Credit Score Do You Need to Buy a House in Canada in 2026?

There is no single credit score that guarantees you can buy a house in Canada in 2026. CMHC's published Purchase requirements state that at least one borrower or guarantor must have a minimum score of 600, but that is one program condition within a complete assessment. It is not a promise that every lender will approve every applicant at 600.

Lenders also assess income, debts, down payment, property, documentation, and their own product requirements. The score visible in your banking app may differ from the one a lender uses. A useful preparation plan therefore improves the accuracy and stability of the entire application rather than chasing a particular number without knowing what the proposed lender requires.

## Understand what the published minimum means

The CMHC threshold relates to eligibility within its mortgage-insurance framework. Mortgage loan insurance and a lender's decision are connected but different assessments. A lender can have additional requirements, and other products or insurers may use different criteria. Ask which lender, insurer, and product are being considered before applying a published minimum to your situation.

The wording at least one borrower or guarantor also should not be interpreted as permission to ignore everyone else on an application. Other applicants, obligations, and credit information remain relevant to underwriting. Adding a person with a higher score does not automatically erase another person's debts or resolve concerns about repayment capacity.

If you see an online statement that every Canadian buyer needs exactly 680, 700, or another number, ask what product and policy it describes. Those figures may reflect a particular lender's preferences rather than a universal legal threshold. Obtain current criteria for the proposed financing instead of treating a general article as an approval decision.

## Separate the score from the credit report

A credit score summarizes information using a particular model. A credit report contains the underlying account history and other recorded details. Two applicants with similar scores can have different histories, balances, or recent events. Reading the report helps you understand what needs explanation or correction before a mortgage application.

Canadian consumer scores commonly use a 300-to-900 scale, but the exact calculation is not public and different models can produce different results. A score from a consumer service is useful for monitoring, yet it may not match the lender's model or the date of its assessment. Avoid assuming a small displayed change has a predictable approval effect.

Keep a dated copy of the reports you review and note any unresolved questions. Your goal is to understand the file, not merely to capture a favourable screenshot. If an account balance or payment history looks wrong, investigate the underlying record rather than searching for a different app that displays a higher number.

## Check both major Canadian credit files

FCAC explains how to obtain credit reports from Equifax and TransUnion. Reviewing your own report does not affect your score. Check both because the information available to each may differ. Use the official access routes and distinguish a free report or disclosure from an optional paid monitoring service.

Compare identifying details, account ownership, balances, payment records, and unfamiliar inquiries. An old address alone may have a different significance from a loan you never opened, but both deserve understanding. Make a short list of issues with the account name, the specific entry, and the evidence needed to investigate it.

Start well before you intend to make an offer. You may need time to gather statements, contact a provider, or follow a bureau's dispute procedure. Do not rely on an assumed correction deadline that has not been confirmed. An unresolved issue is easier to manage when it is discovered before a transaction depends on financing.

## Correct errors through the proper process

If you believe information is inaccurate, gather supporting statements, receipts, or correspondence and dispute the entry through the relevant bureau and reporting organization. Explain the specific correction requested. A clear factual dispute is more useful than a general request to raise the score because you want to qualify for a mortgage.

FCAC states that credit bureaus must correct errors for free. That does not mean accurate negative information can be removed simply because a payment has now been made or an application is approaching. Ask what record will be updated and confirm the result on the relevant report rather than assuming one conversation resolved both bureau files.

If an account may involve fraud, follow the official reporting and protection process promptly. Keep copies of the case details and explain the situation to the professional assessing your application when relevant. Do not submit altered statements or conceal the issue. A genuine correction needs a reliable documentation trail, not a cleaner-looking document created for approval.

## Make payment reliability the first priority

Pay required obligations on time and address a possible shortfall before the due date. A system that records due dates, sends reminders, and maintains enough account cash can be more useful than a complicated score-tracking routine. Automatic payments still need monitoring because insufficient funds or a changed bill amount can interrupt the process.

If you cannot pay an account in full, understand the required minimum and contact the provider about difficulties. Do not deliberately miss a payment while disputing an amount unless the provider's confirmed arrangement addresses the obligation. Keep written confirmation of any agreed payment terms and check subsequent statements for accuracy.

There is no guaranteed number of points attached to one on-time payment, and no responsible preparation plan can promise a precise score by a chosen date. Focus on repeatable behaviour and an affordable budget. A mortgage application benefits from finances that remain manageable after the score has been checked.

## Understand credit utilization without borrowing unnecessarily

Utilization compares revolving balances with available limits. For an invented example, a $1,200 balance against a $6,000 limit represents 20% utilization. A $4,800 balance against the same limit represents 80%. These calculations describe usage; they do not predict the exact score effect or establish a lender's approval decision.

FCAC suggests trying to use less than 30% of total available credit as a general management guideline. Treat it as guidance rather than a mortgage approval threshold or a reason to carry interest-bearing debt. Reducing a balance can improve the financial position even when the resulting score change is uncertain.

Before using savings to repay a balance, preserve funds required for the purchase, closing expenses, and an appropriate reserve. Ask the lender how repayment would affect the actual qualification calculation. A lower revolving balance and a larger down payment can influence the application in different ways, so compare complete scenarios using the same available cash.

## Keep the debt-ratio calculation separate

Creditworthiness and payment capacity are different. A strong score does not create income to support a larger payment. Canadian mortgage assessments commonly consider housing and total-debt ratios using accepted gross income, qualifying mortgage payments, property costs, and other obligations. Those inputs can limit financing even when the credit file appears strong.

For a fictional illustration, assume $8,000 gross monthly accepted income and $3,000 qualifying housing costs. Housing uses 37.5% of that income. If other qualifying debt adds $600 monthly, total obligations become $3,600, or 45%. A high score does not change that arithmetic or automatically create an exception to the applicable product rules.

Conversely, a low debt ratio does not make every credit concern irrelevant. An application can have manageable payments and still need explanation of recent missed obligations or an unresolved record. Prepare both parts of the file: a reliable account history and a mortgage amount supported by documented income and costs.

## Coordinate new credit with the home-buying timeline

Before opening a new account, financing a vehicle, or adding a major installment purchase, consider its effect on required payments and the mortgage assessment. The issue is broader than an inquiry's possible score effect. A new obligation can reduce the income available under the total-debt calculation or change the lender's view of your commitments.

Ask a prospective lender or broker how mortgage shopping and credit checks will be handled. Understand whether an initial discussion uses a soft or hard inquiry and what consent you are providing. There is no need to submit applications indiscriminately just to discover which service might display the most encouraging result.

After a preliminary assessment, report material financial changes and ask whether the approval needs updating. A preapproval does not mean the file can never be reassessed. Keep balances and documents current, and avoid assuming that a decision based on last month's obligations automatically covers a different situation at closing.

## Prepare when Canadian credit history is limited

Limited history is different from a history showing missed payments, but either may require additional assessment. CMHC notes that alternative ways to establish creditworthiness may be considered for borrowers without a credit history. That possibility depends on the product and documentation; it is not a guaranteed substitute accepted by every lender.

If you are new to Canada, ask what records the proposed lender can evaluate and how foreign history is treated. An international report, banking reference, or other evidence may be relevant in some situations. Do not assume that a score from another country translates directly into a Canadian score or satisfies a particular mortgage policy.

Rent-payment records can be useful evidence of financial behaviour where the lender accepts them, but keeping records does not by itself guarantee reporting to a bureau or mortgage approval. Ask exactly what the lender needs. Save genuine lease and payment documentation rather than purchasing a service on the assumption that it automatically solves an application requirement.

## Treat alternative financing as a complete cost decision

If a proposed lender cannot approve the application, ask which condition is preventing approval before moving to another product. The issue may involve income documentation, debt, property, or available cash rather than the score alone. A clear explanation helps distinguish a problem that can be resolved from a product mismatch.

If considering alternative or private financing, review the full rate, fees, term, payment requirements, and repayment or renewal plan. A possible approval is not enough to establish affordability. Model what happens when the term ends and identify realistic sources for repayment instead of relying on an unconfirmed future refinance.

Compare delaying the purchase with proceeding under more expensive terms. Waiting may allow more savings, lower balances, or a longer documented history, but it also has housing and timing costs. Write down both scenarios. The appropriate decision depends on actual offers and household needs rather than the pressure to obtain approval at any price.

## Build a practical mortgage-readiness routine

Begin with report accuracy, a complete debt list, current income documents, and evidence for the down payment. Then ask a lender or broker to identify the relevant credit requirements and remaining conditions. This creates a useful action list instead of an open-ended target to improve a score without knowing what would change the application.

Review progress at a manageable interval and after important corrections. Track balances, payment reliability, savings, and unresolved conditions alongside the score. A higher number is only one possible sign of progress. Having verified closing cash and a smaller required debt payment may matter more to a specific application.

Set the purchase budget using the complete after-tax cost of ownership. Include insurance, maintenance, utilities, taxes, and the cash needed after moving. Mortgage readiness means both being eligible for suitable financing and being able to carry it. A successful credit check should support a sustainable home purchase rather than become the sole objective.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 600 enough to get a mortgage in 2026?

It meets the stated minimum-score condition for at least one borrower or guarantor under the cited CMHC Purchase requirements, but it does not guarantee a lender's approval. Other criteria and lender policies still apply. Ask for the current requirements of the actual product, using the score model and full application the lender will assess.

### Will checking my own score hurt it?

FCAC states that checking your own report or score does not affect your rating. A lender's application-related inquiry is different. Understand which process is being used and obtain your own records through legitimate access routes. Reviewing information early can reveal errors or missing details before they complicate a mortgage application.

### Can I guarantee a higher score before buying?

No fixed improvement can be promised because models, reporting timing, and individual files differ. Correct inaccurate records, maintain affordable payments, manage balances, and avoid unnecessary borrowing. Use AffordBase's debt-ratio and down-payment guides to prepare the wider application, then obtain an assessment based on actual lender criteria rather than an advertised score guarantee.
