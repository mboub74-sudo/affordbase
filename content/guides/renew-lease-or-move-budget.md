Should you renew your lease or move to a cheaper apartment? Start with the total cost over the months you expect to stay, then check the cash needed before moving day. A lower advertised rent can be attractive while still leaving you worse off after movers, overlapping leases, commuting changes, and setup charges.

This rent planning guide gives Canadian and US renters a practical comparison they can repeat with their own quotes. You will calculate a moving break-even point, separate refundable deposits from permanent expenses, and test whether either apartment leaves room for ordinary bills and savings. All dollar examples are fictional. Use Canadian dollars for a Canadian household and US dollars for a US household; the examples are not exchange-rate comparisons.

## Quick answer: compare two totals before deciding

Build one figure for the recurring monthly cost of staying and another for moving. Include rent, mandatory fees, utilities, insurance, parking, and any change in transportation. Multiply each by the same comparison period. Then add the nonrefundable costs required for that option and subtract any confirmed concessions that apply during that period.

Separately calculate the largest cash amount you must provide before reimbursements or deposit refunds arrive. An option can have the lower twelve-month cost but require more upfront money than you can safely access. Treat both tests as necessary: the move should make sense over your expected stay, and its timing should fit your actual bank balance.

## Begin with the renewal offer and your deadline

Collect the proposed renewal rent, proposed start date, lease length, included services, and every recurring charge. Compare the written offer with your existing lease. Ask whether parking, storage, internet, or an amenity charge changes separately from base rent. A small rent increase combined with a new monthly fee can create a larger budget change than the headline suggests.

Record the date when you need to respond and the notice required if you leave. Do not assume that the end of a fixed term automatically ends every obligation, or that silence always renews a tenancy on identical terms. Requirements vary with the jurisdiction and the lease. Give yourself enough time to obtain local guidance before making a commitment you cannot reverse easily.

For Canada, start with your provincial or territorial tenancy authority. For the United States, check the applicable state and local housing rules. These checks establish the options available to you; the calculations in this guide compare their financial consequences. They do not establish whether a particular increase, fee, deposit, or notice is lawful.

## Use take-home pay for the household cash test

Income percentages can help screen listings, but your spending plan needs the money available after payroll deductions. Write down reliable monthly take-home income and subtract nonhousing essentials, required debt payments, annual-bill reserves, and a savings contribution you want to maintain. The amount remaining is your household housing capacity before a flexibility buffer.

Suppose take-home income is $4,600. Nonhousing living costs and debt payments total $1,750, annual-bill reserves are $200, and planned savings are $450. That leaves $2,200 before an additional buffer. Choosing an all-in housing cost of $2,190 would leave only $10 of unassigned cash, so the apparent affordability is fragile even though the subtraction technically works.

Gross-income guidelines answer a different question. FCAC publishes a general Canadian benchmark for rent and household expenses, while landlords may apply their own screening policies within applicable rules. Neither establishes a universal personal spending limit. Use the [rent affordability calculator](/rent-affordability-calculator/) to explore an initial range, then replace estimates with your household figures.

## Build an all-in monthly comparison

Put comparable costs on matching rows rather than comparing one apartment's rent with another apartment's complete package. Ask what is included in each quoted amount. If a unit includes heating, do not add the same heating estimate again; if it excludes electricity, do not quietly assume your current electricity bill will remain unchanged.

| Monthly item | Renew current apartment | Move to another apartment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base rent | $1,950 | $1,750 |
| Utilities | $130 | $170 |
| Internet | $60 | $60 |
| Tenant or renters insurance | $25 | $25 |
| Parking and mandatory fees | $75 | $90 |
| Transportation | $110 | $160 |
| Comparable monthly total | $2,350 | $2,255 |

The alternative apartment saves $200 in base rent but only $95 in this full comparison. These are household assumptions, not city averages or price quotes. The transportation row includes the costs that differ between locations; use the same boundary for both options so the comparison does not accidentally favor one address.

Check utility seasonality, laundry charges, storage needs, and whether a new location requires a vehicle. A lower rent can disappear inside a longer commute. Conversely, an apartment near work may justify a higher rent if it eliminates substantial transportation spending. Compare the complete arrangement you would actually use rather than an ideal version of the cheapest listing.

## Calculate nonrefundable moving costs

Obtain quotes for movers or a rental truck, packing materials, cleaning, utility connection charges, and essential replacement purchases. Include any verified lease overlap and permitted application or administrative costs. Count income you would realistically lose while moving if you cannot use paid time off. Avoid treating an optional furniture upgrade as a requirement for every move.

For the example, assume moving and packing cost $700, overlapping rent costs $650, cleaning and setup cost $200, and necessary replacements cost $160. The total permanent moving expense is $1,710. Divide that by the $95 recurring monthly saving: $1,710 divided by $95 equals eighteen months to recover the outlay.

Break-even months equal incremental nonrefundable moving costs divided by recurring monthly savings. This simple formula assumes the monthly difference remains constant and excludes financing costs and investment returns. If the new option costs more each month, there is no financial break-even from monthly savings under those assumptions. You may still value the move for other reasons, but label that tradeoff clearly.

## Compare twelve months and your likely stay

At twelve months, renewing costs $2,350 multiplied by twelve, or $28,200. Moving costs $2,255 multiplied by twelve plus $1,710, or $28,770. The supposedly cheaper apartment costs $570 more over the first year in this scenario. That does not mean staying is always better; it identifies the period over which this particular move becomes worthwhile.

At twenty-four months with unchanged costs, staying totals $56,400 and moving totals $55,830. The move is now $570 cheaper. Real renewals, utility charges, and commuting arrangements may change, so this is a comparison framework rather than a forecast. Recalculate if either lease ends before the period you are using or a concession expires along the way.

Use the time you reasonably expect to remain in the apartment. A possible job relocation next spring makes a three-year saving less persuasive. When your future is uncertain, compare shorter and longer stays side by side. This makes the decision depend on visible assumptions rather than the emotionally appealing rent number in an advertisement.

## Keep refundable deposits in a cash timeline

A refundable deposit is generally cash tied up subject to the agreement and local rules, rather than automatically a permanent expense. Do not put its full amount into moving cost and also count its eventual return as ordinary income. Keep it on a separate cash schedule, including possible deductions and uncertainty about when the money will arrive.

Imagine the new apartment requires an additional $1,200 refundable deposit before your old deposit is returned. You may need $1,710 of moving expenses plus $1,200 of temporary deposit funding, or $2,910, before considering the regular rent payments already due. The permanent moving expense remains $1,710 if the additional deposit is ultimately returned in full.

Map actual payment dates, including the first rent payment and any prepaid rent, to avoid double counting. Keep the emergency reserve separate. If the schedule only works by assuming an immediate deposit refund or using expensive credit, postpone the decision until the funding gap is resolved. The [moving budget checklist](/articles/moving-budget-checklist/) provides a broader list of transition costs.

## Treat free rent as a dated concession

A free month can reduce first-year cost without reducing the payment due in the other months. If contractual rent is $1,800 for twelve months and one month is genuinely waived, the rent paid over that period is $19,800. Dividing by twelve gives $1,650 of effective monthly rent, but your ordinary paid month may still require $1,800.

Read the conditions. Ask when the credit applies, whether it is already included in the advertised figure, and whether leaving early creates repayment obligations. Keep recurring mandatory charges outside the rent calculation unless the concession explicitly covers them. A promotion should appear once in your worksheet, on the dates the contract actually provides it.

## Negotiate with evidence and realistic alternatives

Bring several comparable current listings to a renewal discussion. Match location, condition, size, included utilities, parking, and lease length. Ask about a lower increase, a parking adjustment, or a useful repair rather than assuming only one number is negotiable. Keep requests polite, specific, and documented so a verbal conversation does not become a budget assumption.

Consider how the proposed agreement fits your plans. A longer commitment may create stability but reduce flexibility if your work or household changes. A one-time credit is valuable differently from a lasting monthly reduction. Compare the written response with the move option, and do not give notice until you understand both the legal timing and the availability of the alternative home.

## Stress-test the choice before you sign

Run a second worksheet with higher moving costs, no unconfirmed concession, and the upper end of utility estimates. In the example, another $300 of moving expense raises break-even from eighteen months to just over twenty-one months. If monthly savings fall from $95 to $60, the original $1,710 takes twenty-eight and a half months to recover.

Also test a temporary income interruption. Determine which bills could continue without using money reserved for food, medication, or required debt payments. A move that leaves no cushion deserves more scrutiny than a move with a slightly smaller saving and ample cash remaining. The cheapest mathematical total is only one part of a sustainable rental decision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I move whenever my landlord raises the rent?

No. Compare the proposed all-in cost of staying with the full moving alternative over your expected stay. A modest increase may cost less than movers, overlapping rent, and a longer commute. Also verify whether the increase and its notice comply with the local rules before treating the offered amount as settled.

### Are security deposits part of the cost of moving?

Separate refundable deposits from expenses you will not recover. They still belong in the upfront cash requirement because you may need to fund the new deposit before receiving the old one. Budget conservatively for timing and potential lawful deductions rather than assuming every dollar returns immediately.

### Can I compare apartments using net effective rent?

Use effective rent to compare a defined period, then separately check the actual payment schedule. Include required fees, utilities, and concession conditions. An apartment advertised at an appealing effective price can still require a higher cash payment during most months, which matters for payday budgeting.

### Is breaking my lease worth it to get cheaper rent?

Only calculate that option after checking your agreement and applicable law, including notice and any continuing obligations. Add verified termination costs to the comparison. If those costs consume the savings before you expect to move again, the financial case is weak even when the alternative rent is lower.

## Your next rental planning step

Write down the renewal deadline, collect actual quotes for at least one alternative, and compare both the first-year total and the peak cash requirement. Save the worksheet with its assumptions. Once you choose, update your household budget and automate any genuine saving instead of allowing a lower rent to disappear into unrelated spending.
