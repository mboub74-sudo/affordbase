Mortgage renewal in Canada is a chance to review your payment, compare contract features, and decide whether your current lender still fits your needs. Begin a few months before maturity rather than treating the renewal letter as a deadline to accept the first offer. The most useful comparison uses your expected balance at renewal, the same remaining amortization, and complete switching costs.

This guide includes an original payment example, a practical shopping timeline, and a household budget check. All rates and household amounts below are hypothetical planning inputs, not advertised offers or forecasts. Rules were checked on November 17, 2026; a lender must confirm the requirements and pricing for your actual mortgage.

## What mortgage renewal means

A mortgage term is the period covered by the current contract. Amortization is the planned time needed to repay the debt. When a term ends, a remaining balance usually needs another contract unless you repay it in full. Renewing for a five-year term does not necessarily mean restarting a twenty-five-year repayment schedule.

Write those two dates separately on your planning sheet. The maturity date tells you when the contract ends. The remaining amortization describes how long repayment is expected to take. A comparison that changes both the interest rate and repayment period can make an offer look cheaper without showing why its payment fell.

Use the renewal balance rather than the original purchase mortgage. If you initially borrowed $420,000 but expect to owe $350,000 at maturity, calculate the new payment on $350,000. Ask for an updated estimate when the date approaches, especially if you plan a permitted lump-sum payment before the new term starts.

## Start with a complete mortgage snapshot

Collect the latest statement, contract, maturity date, rate type, payment frequency, projected balance, and remaining amortization. Record whether property taxes or optional insurance are collected with the mortgage payment. A bank withdrawal that includes those items cannot be compared directly with another lender's principal-and-interest quote.

Also record prepayment privileges, portability conditions, and the method used to calculate an early-exit charge. These details matter if you might sell, move, receive an inheritance, or change jobs. The cheapest rate can be less useful when the contract makes a likely future change expensive or difficult.

Keep a simple document folder containing income records, identification, mortgage information, and property documents requested by prospective lenders. A new lender may assess the application even when the transaction qualifies for a regulatory switching exception. Preparing documents early is easier than trying to assemble them during the final week before maturity.

## A realistic renewal shopping timeline

Three or four months before maturity, establish your budget ceiling and ask lenders or a broker when they can provide an offer or rate hold. This is a suggested planning window, not a promise that every lender will hold a rate for that period. Ask how long an offer lasts and what conditions can change it.

Around two months before maturity, compare written proposals using the same balance, payment frequency, and remaining repayment period. Ask your existing lender whether it can improve its offer. Keep dated notes so you do not compare an expired proposal with a newly available one and assume both remain accessible.

As maturity approaches, confirm acceptance deadlines, discharge instructions, legal work if needed, and the date of the first new payment. FCAC states that federally regulated institutions must provide a renewal statement, or notice of non-renewal, at least twenty-one days before the term ends. That minimum notice period is not a reason to delay shopping.

## Calculate the payment increase using consistent inputs

Consider a fictional household with a $350,000 mortgage balance and twenty years remaining. The table holds that balance and remaining period constant. Payments are monthly principal and interest, using nominal annual rates compounded semi-annually, a common Canadian fixed-mortgage convention. Product conventions and rounding may produce different lender figures.

| Illustrative annual rate | Monthly payment | Difference from 2.50% scenario |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2.50% | $1,852.46 | Reference only |
| 4.50% | $2,206.42 | $353.96 more |
| 5.50% | $2,395.37 | $542.91 more |
| 6.50% | $2,591.75 | $739.29 more |

The 2.50% row is a same-balance reference, not a reconstruction of this household's historical payment. Its actual old payment depends on the original loan and repayment history. Use the amount on your statement when measuring your personal increase. This distinction prevents a neat example from becoming a misleading claim about every renewing borrower.

For reproducibility, convert nominal annual rate j to monthly rate i using i = (1 + j/2)^(1/6) - 1. Then calculate payment as balance × i ÷ [1 - (1 + i)^(-number of monthly payments)]. Here, the payment count is 240. The figures exclude taxes, insurance, fees, and changes during the term.

## Translate the increase into a household budget

Assume the household brings home $6,500 monthly. Its non-mortgage essential spending is $2,800, planned savings and irregular-bill reserves total $900, and flexible spending is $600. Those allocations leave $2,200 for principal and interest. A quoted payment of $2,395.37 would exceed the current plan by $195.37 each month.

That shortfall needs an explicit adjustment. The household could identify a sustainable reduction in flexible spending, revisit the timing of a discretionary goal, or consider a different suitable offer. It should not quietly remove annual insurance, repairs, or necessary medication from the worksheet to make the mortgage line fit.

Test an imperfect month too. If take-home income temporarily drops by $400, the same arrangement requires another response. A reserve can absorb a temporary disruption, but routine withdrawals to support an unaffordable recurring payment are a different problem. The [annual budgeting guide](/articles/how-to-create-an-annual-budget/) helps reveal bills that monthly averages can hide.

## Compare more than the advertised interest rate

Create a comparison sheet with one column per offer. Record the term, rate type, payment, amortization, prepayment permissions, portability, fees, and any conditions attached to a cashback or promotional benefit. Ask whether charges are paid now, added to the balance, reimbursed later, or recoverable if you leave early.

A simple cash-flow screening calculation can help. Suppose a switch costs $900 upfront and reduces the monthly payment by $45. Dividing $900 by $45 gives twenty months to recover that cash outlay through payment savings. This is only a screening tool: a full comparison also includes interest, remaining balances, and any difference in contract features.

Ask for the balance projected at the end of the proposed term. Two offers can have similar monthly payments while repaying different amounts of principal. Comparing cash paid plus the remaining debt gives a clearer picture than ranking offers by the payment alone. Use the same assumed time horizon for both proposals.

## Understand switching and stress-test limits

OSFI says it does not prescribe the minimum qualifying rate for an eligible uninsured straight switch at renewal between federally regulated lenders when the loan amount and remaining contractual amortization do not increase. That is a specific exception, not a blanket exemption for every renewal, refinance, lender, or mortgage change.

The receiving institution still assesses creditworthiness and repayment capacity under its underwriting policies. Ask whether your transaction qualifies, what documents are needed, and whether any requested change takes it outside the exception. Insured mortgages and lenders outside federal regulation may involve different requirements; obtain confirmation for the actual product.

Use the [Canadian mortgage stress-test guide](/articles/mortgage-stress-test-canada/) to understand the wider qualification framework. Do not assume that being allowed to apply without one prescribed rate means automatic approval. Equally, do not rule out shopping solely because you have heard that every switch always requires the same stress test.

## Be careful when extending amortization

At the illustrative 5.50% rate, $350,000 over twenty years produces a monthly payment of about $2,395.37. Extending the calculation to twenty-five years reduces it to about $2,136.37, approximately $259 less monthly. That lower payment is partly the result of repaying the balance more slowly, not a better interest rate.

The extension may improve immediate cash flow, but it can increase interest costs and change qualification or insurance requirements. Request a side-by-side schedule showing payments and balances at the next renewal. Do not compare a twenty-five-year offer against a twenty-year offer without highlighting that difference prominently on the sheet.

If relief is needed, ask about available options before missing payments. Availability depends on circumstances and the lender; approval is not guaranteed. FCAC's mortgage-relief guidance explains potential approaches, including changes that defer principal or add amounts to the balance. Understand how each proposal affects interest and future obligations before agreeing.

## Consider lump sums without emptying your reserve

A permitted prepayment can reduce the balance on which the renewal payment is calculated. Ask the lender to quote the payment with and without the intended amount and confirm the timing and penalty rules. A calculation based on a lower balance is useful only if you can actually make that payment under the contract.

For example, allocating $10,000 to the mortgage leaves $10,000 less available for emergencies or upcoming repairs. If the household then borrows on a high-interest card after a roof leak, the apparent improvement can be undermined. List known expenses and reserve needs before deciding how much cash is genuinely available for prepayment.

Compare that choice with other debts using actual rates and required payments. The [emergency fund versus debt guide](/articles/emergency-fund-vs-paying-off-debt/) provides a framework for balancing resilience and repayment. Neither using every dollar for the mortgage nor keeping every dollar in savings is automatically appropriate for every household.

## Questions to ask before signing

Ask the lender to confirm the exact payment, the balance being renewed, payment dates, remaining amortization, and all fees. Request an explanation of the early-exit charge using a hypothetical future sale date. Ask whether optional products are included and what happens if you decline them. Keep the answers with the written offer.

If considering variable rates, ask how payments respond to rate changes under that specific product. Some products change the payment; others can change how much goes toward principal. Use an adverse scenario instead of assuming the starting payment remains comfortable. A general prediction about future rates cannot replace that contract-specific explanation.

Finally, verify the administrative handoff. Confirm which lender will collect the next payment, whether tax collection arrangements change, and whether any documents remain outstanding. After completion, inspect the first statement for the agreed rate, balance, payment, and fees. A signed offer is useful evidence if an implementation error needs correcting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to renew with my current bank?

No. You can compare other lenders, subject to their assessment and the costs of switching. Begin early enough to handle documents and any legal or administrative work. Ask the current lender for its best written proposal too, then compare the complete terms rather than only the advertised rate.

### Is mortgage renewal the same as refinancing?

Not necessarily. A straightforward renewal continues financing at term end, while refinancing may change the amount borrowed or other loan conditions. The label alone is not enough: ask what is changing and how that affects qualification, fees, insurance, and repayment. Do not assume every change receives straight-switch treatment.

### Can I lower my payment without getting a lower rate?

Sometimes a lower balance or longer amortization reduces the payment, but those choices have different consequences. A lump sum uses savings, while an extension slows repayment and may increase interest. Request comparable schedules and check your cash reserve before treating a lower monthly amount as a cheaper overall mortgage.

### What if the new payment is unaffordable?

Contact the lender before a missed payment and explain the shortfall using a complete budget. Ask about available relief and its longer-term costs. Review essentials, debt obligations, and realistic housing choices. No general article can guarantee a concession, but early communication gives more time to evaluate actual options.

### When should I start preparing for renewal?

Find the exact maturity date on your mortgage statement, then begin comparing options a few months before it. This gives you time to request offers, prepare documents, and understand switching costs. Ask each lender about offer expiry dates and processing deadlines. Do not assume the renewal letter provides enough time to complete a switch comfortably.

### Which balance and amortization should I use?

Use the balance projected for your renewal date and the remaining amortization, rather than the original loan amount. Include any planned prepayment only after confirming it is permitted and affordable. Compare offers using the same balance, repayment period, and payment frequency so a lower payment is not simply the result of extending your debt over more years.

### Should I choose a fixed or variable rate?

Compare your need for payment certainty with your ability to absorb higher costs. A fixed rate stays unchanged during its term, while a variable rate can change; whether the payment changes depends on the product. Ask for higher-rate scenarios and early-exit costs. Choose using your budget and actual contract terms rather than relying only on predictions about the market.
